Board of Supervisors · Week of July 17, 2026
The big picture
San Francisco's Board of Supervisors advanced a sweeping set of consequential items on July 14, continuing the two-year city budget to a final vote on July 21 while passing on first reading a major reduction in affordable housing requirements — with a hard-fought Mission District carve-out surviving a 6–5 amendment vote. The Board also approved placing two significant charter amendments before November voters: one to extend and expand the Housing Trust Fund through 2058, and another to consolidate and restructure dozens of city commissions. Cannabis cafés received final approval in a 7–4 vote, and drug-free permanent supportive housing became city policy by the same margin.
Top items
IN PROGRESS
City budget heads to final vote next week after last-minute amendments
The Board amended and then continued on first reading the two-year city budget and salary ordinance for fiscal years 2026–2028, adding a Sheriff's Office budget increase of nearly $3.7 million and language on climate-related job training. A companion measure adjusting homelessness housing subsidies was also continued, with the Board settling on 750 rapid rehousing slots and 50 long-term subsidies for single-room-occupancy families instead of the originally proposed 800 rapid rehousing slots.
The two-year spending plan governs all city department budgets through June 2028. The last-minute shift in homelessness housing subsidies — trading 50 rapid rehousing slots for long-term support for single-room-occupancy families — signals ongoing negotiation over how the city houses its most vulnerable residents.
All budget-related items continued unanimously to the July 21, 2026 Board meeting for final action.
IN PROGRESS
Affordable housing fees cut citywide, with Mission District carve-out added
After four rounds of rescinded votes, the Board passed on first reading a major overhaul of the city's Inclusionary Affordable Housing Program, reducing the share of affordable units required in large developments and eliminating requirements for projects under 25 units. A last-minute amendment by Supervisor Fielder, approved 6–5, carved out the Mission Area Plan with a higher 8% on-site affordability requirement for both rental and ownership projects.
The ordinance reduces the cost burden on housing developers citywide in an effort to spur construction, but the contested Mission District carve-out — requiring 8% affordable units at income levels targeting households earning up to 120% of Area Median Income — reflects deep disagreement about whether fee reductions will actually produce affordable homes in one of the city's most displacement-pressured neighborhoods.
Passed on first reading 9–2 (Chen and Walton dissenting) after multiple vote rescissions; requires a second reading to become law.
IN PROGRESS
Voters to decide on boosting and extending the city's Housing Trust Fund
The Board unanimously approved placing a charter amendment on the November 3, 2026 ballot that would increase annual city contributions to the Housing Trust Fund, extend the fund's life from 2043 to 2058, and allow temporary reductions in contributions under defined fiscal stress conditions.
The Housing Trust Fund is the city's primary dedicated source of financing for affordable rental and ownership housing, down-payment assistance, and housing infrastructure. Extending it by 15 years and increasing annual contributions would significantly expand the city's long-term affordable housing capacity — if voters approve.
Ordered submitted to the November 2026 ballot, 11–0.
IN PROGRESS
Voters to decide on sweeping overhaul of city commissions and boards
The Board voted 9–2 to place a charter amendment on the November 2026 ballot that would consolidate, eliminate, or restructure dozens of city commissions and advisory bodies, transfer some from the City Charter to the Municipal Code, and give the City Administrator new authority over real estate transactions. The measure also shifts Board approval of legal settlements from ordinances to resolutions, streamlining the process.
The amendment represents one of the most significant restructurings of San Francisco's governance apparatus in years, potentially eliminating or merging bodies that currently provide public oversight of city departments. Supervisors Chan and Fielder dissented, suggesting concerns about reduced accountability.
Ordered submitted to the November 2026 ballot, 9–2 (Chan and Fielder dissenting).
IN PROGRESS
Drug-free permanent supportive housing becomes city policy in 7–4 vote
The Board passed on first reading an ordinance making it city policy to expand drug-free permanent supportive housing for people experiencing homelessness, requiring that new city-funded housing meet drug-free standards unless legally prohibited, and directing the Department of Homelessness and Supportive Housing to survey residents about their preferences and establish eviction protocols.
The ordinance marks a significant policy shift in how the city funds and operates supportive housing, prioritizing drug-free environments for new placements. Critics who voted no raised concerns about whether the policy could restrict housing access for people who use drugs and need shelter.
Passed on first reading 7–4 (Chan, Chen, Fielder, and Walton dissenting); a competing amendment to remove shelter from the policy's scope failed 5–6.
